1. Introduction

1.1 Background

Nurses face a range of workplace challenges, including high workloads, work-related stress and burnout. A meta-analysis conducted by Ge et al. (2023), involving 94 studies across more than 30 countries, estimated the global prevalence of nursing burnout at 30%. Similarly, a study of 1,826 nurses at Singapore General Hospital found that 39% experienced high emotional exhaustion, 40% high depersonalisation, and 59% low personal accomplishment (Ang et al., 2016). Furthermore, Kiptulon et al. (2024) identified several factors contributing to work-related stress among nurses, including understaffing, role conflict, bullying, and a lack of support from supervisors and colleagues. Their systematic review also found that positive organisational cultures, characterised by effective communication, teamwork, and supportive leadership, were associated with lower levels of work-related stress.

These findings highlight the importance of providing nurses with a supportive working environment to help them manage workplace challenges and maintain their well-being. A study of 900 nurses in the United States also found that work engagement was significantly associated with job satisfaction (p < 0.001) (Wei et al., 2023). As healthcare systems face increasing workforce demands, supportive leadership approaches such as coaching may help address the evolving needs of nursing staff. Unlike traditional directive leadership approaches, coaching leadership emphasises empathy, collaboration and a growth-oriented mindset. Rather than giving direct instructions or solutions, nurse managers can use coaching techniques to encourage staff nurses to reflect on their experiences, develop critical thinking and problem-solving skills, and take greater ownership of their professional development (Wei et al., 2026).

Effective communication is essential to nursing practice, enabling nurse managers to support staff development, address workplace challenges, and foster a positive work environment (Meneses-La-Riva et al., 2025). Nurse managers also play an important role in guiding staff nurses through challenges such as workplace conflicts, work-related stress and professional development. Therefore, having effective coaching skills can help nurse managers support their staff in handling these situations. Unlike traditional directive leadership approaches, coaching leadership emphasises active listening, reflective questioning, and helping individuals develop their own solutions. By adopting a coaching approach, nurse managers can encourage staff nurses to reflect on their experiences and identify areas for improvement, while also helping them build confidence, improve communication, and take greater ownership of their professional development (Richardson et al., 2023).

To promote a coaching-oriented leadership culture, the National University Hospital (NUH) incorporates coaching principles into its Exemplary Leader Coaching Programme (ELCP) through a one-day masterclass. In the masterclass, nurse managers learn coaching mindsets, communication techniques, and the 3P and GROW-L frameworks. The 3P framework includes Partner, Practice and Process. The GROW-L framework guides coaching conversations through five stages, which are Goal, Reality, Options, Will, and Learning.

While the ELCP provides nurse managers with a foundation in coaching principles, they still need repeated practice, reflection, and feedback to develop their coaching and communication skills. Applying these techniques in the workplace can be challenging, especially when nurse managers face sensitive conversations or staff nurses who resist feedback. Nurse managers used to giving direct instruction may also struggle to change their communication habits. Therefore, repeated coaching practice can help nurse managers become more familiar with techniques they have learnt and apply them more effectively in real workplace conversations.

1.2 Problem statement

Nurse managers who undergo NUH's ELCP are taught structured coaching principles and skills through the one-day masterclass and are expected to apply these skills in workplace coaching conversations. However, they may have limited opportunities to practise coaching conversations before applying these skills with actual staff nurses. They may also have limited opportunities to receive immediate feedback on their coaching approach. As a result, nurse managers may find it challenging to apply the coaching techniques they have learnt in real workplace conversations.

1.3 Value proposition

The proposed AI-based coaching simulator aims to provide nurse managers with an on-demand platform to practise coaching conversations based on the principles introduced during the ELCP masterclass. In the coaching simulator, nurse managers will act as a coach and interact with an AI staff nurse acting as the coachee. The AI staff nurse will respond to the nurse manager based on different coaching scenarios, allowing nurse managers to practise handling different workplace situations. After each conversation, the system will provide structured feedback based on the 3P and GROW-L frameworks to help nurse managers identify their strengths and areas for improvement. The system also allows nurse managers to retry scenarios and try different responses at their own pace. Through repeated practice and feedback, the simulator helps nurse managers to build confidence before applying the techniques they have learnt in real workplace conversations.

1.4 Objectives and Scope

The main objective of this project is to develop an AI-based coaching simulator that allows nurse managers to practise coaching conversations with virtual staff nurses across different scenarios.

The specific objectives of this project are:

  • To develop an AI-based conversation system that allows nurse managers to interact with AI staff nurses in different coaching scenarios
  • To develop an AI-based assessment system based on the 3P and GROW-L frameworks to evaluate the nurse managers’ coaching skills and provide structured feedback on areas demonstrated well and on areas that can be improved
  • To develop both web-based and virtual reality (VR) interfaces that allow nurse managers to practise coaching conversations
  • To incorporate emotional responses and facial expressions into the virtual staff nurse to make coaching more realistic

This project focuses on coaching conversations between a nurse manager and an AI staff nurse. The simulator is a coaching tool that assesses only the nurse manager’s coaching skills, while the AI staff nurse responds based on the conversation. The simulator is not meant to replace NUH’s existing ELCP masterclass. It is for nurse managers to practise the coaching techniques they learnt in the masterclass and to build confidence before applying them in real workplace conversations.

2. Background Research

2.1 Primary research

To gain a deeper insight into the problem, regular meetings were conducted with the head nurse at NUH from September 2026 onwards. The purpose was to understand how coaching skills are trained and assessed in the hospital and what practice tools are needed to help nurse managers improve their coaching skills. The project initially explored AI-supported practice for nursing communication with patients, but these meetings helped narrow its focus to supporting nurse managers in developing and improving their coaching skills when engaging with nursing staff. These discussions also allowed us to clarify project requirements and ensure that the proposed solution remained aligned with NUH’s expectations as the project progressed.

During the discussions, the NUH head nurse highlighted that participation in the ELCP masterclass alone may not provide sufficient opportunities for nurse managers to develop confidence in applying the coaching skills. Although the masterclass introduces multiple relevant coaching principles and frameworks, additional opportunities for repeated practice may be necessary to reinforce these skills and support their application in workplace conversations.

2.2 Literature review

Developing effective coaching skills requires more than just an understanding of coaching principles, as individuals must also be able to apply them in practice. Grant (2007) compared a 13-week coaching training programme involving 23 participants with a 2-day intensive programme involving 20 participants. Both programmes significantly improved participants' coaching skills, but the 13-week programme produced a larger improvement of 7.3 compared with 3.1 in the 2-day programme. Furthermore, only the 13-week programme showed improvements in emotional intelligence. These findings suggest that extended coaching training may provide additional benefits compared with shorter intensive programmes, highlighting the potential value of continued coaching development beyond initial training.

One commonly used approach to developing interpersonal communication skills is role-play, which provides learners with opportunities to apply theoretical knowledge in a simulated setting. Through role-plays, learners can practise different scenarios, receive feedback, and reflect on their performance in a controlled environment. Two commonly used forms of traditional role-play are peer role-play and role-play involving standardised participants (SPs).

In peer role-play, learners take turns to be the coach and coachee, allowing them to experience both roles and understand each other's perspectives (Paramasivan & Khoo, 2020). While this is a low-cost and easily accessible approach, the realism of the scenario and the quality of the feedback depend on the partner’s acting ability and coaching experience. Furthermore, learners may feel self-conscious practising in front of colleagues, potentially discouraging them from experimenting with new coaching techniques.

SPs, on the other hand, provide realistic scenarios and structured feedback, which addresses the limitations of peer role-play. However, providing consistent feedback remains a challenge, as even trained SPs may experience difficulty in delivering constructive and specific feedback to learners. According to a nationwide survey involving 250 SPs conducted by Abe et al. (2011), 56.8% reported difficulty with 4 or more feedback-related tasks, such as giving positive feedback when a learner performed poorly or distinguishing between several learners' interviews when giving individual feedback. Furthermore, engaging SPs is resource-intensive, as it requires funding, training, and coordinated scheduling (Sehgal et al., 2025). As a result, these limitations may limit the accessibility and frequency of practice opportunities.

AI-based conversational simulations have become a potential approach to overcoming some of the limitations of traditional role-plays. Unlike conventional methods that require the involvement of peers or trained participants, AI-based simulation allows learners to practise independently and repeatedly, providing greater flexibility and accessibility. Powered by large language models (LLMs) and speech technologies, these systems can simulate interactive conversations with virtual characters and have been explored in healthcare education to support communication training (Herschbach et al., 2025).

Beyond simulating conversations, AI-based systems can also provide structured feedback to support skill development. Depending on their capabilities, these systems can analyse conversational content, vocal tone, and non-verbal cues to evaluate different aspects of communication (Stamer et al., 2023). Such feedback may help learners to identify their strengths and areas for improvement, supporting reflection and continued practice.

Despite these potential benefits, AI-based simulations still face several limitations. The most commonly reported barrier is lack of authenticity. Virtual patients are not able to adapt to the context of a conversation, handle small talk, or sustain a natural flow of conversation, and their responses can feel limited and predictable, which some learners found unnatural (Stamer et al., 2023). Furthermore, Li et al. (2024) highlighted challenges associated with using LLMs to simulate patients, such as role reversal, hallucinations and repetitive responses. These issues may affect the consistency and realism of simulated interactions, highlighting the need for mechanisms that guide the LLMs’ behaviour throughout the conversation.

Beyond these practical limitations, evidence regarding the effectiveness of AI-based communication simulations remains limited. Although these systems show potential for supporting communication skills training, further evaluation is needed to establish their effectiveness and reliability (Jo et al., 2025).

2.3 Research gaps

Based on the primary research and literature review, several gaps have been identified to inform the development of the proposed AI coaching simulator. Firstly, although NUH’s ELCP masterclass introduces the principles and techniques of coaching, there is a need for additional opportunities to practise and apply these skills in realistic workplace conversations. Traditional role-play methods may provide valuable learning experiences. However, they are limited by the availability of participants, facilitators, and resources.

Secondly, while AI-based simulations offer opportunities for repeated practice, previous research has highlighted their limitations in conversational authenticity and natural dialogue. This highlights the need to investigate whether AI-generated responses can realistically represent the emotional and behavioural responses of nursing staff during challenging coaching conversations.

Lastly, although AI-based systems can provide automated coaching feedback, the accuracy and suitability of such feedback for NUH’s specific coaching requirements remain to be established. In particular, it is necessary to evaluate whether AI-generated assessments are able to reflect the GROW-L and 3P frameworks used in the ELCP masterclass.

Therefore, this project aims to develop an AI-based coaching simulator tailored to NUH’s training requirements, providing nurse managers with opportunities to practise realistic coaching conversations and receive structured feedback aligned with the GROW-L and 3P frameworks.

3.6 Constraints

  • Cost: each session uses four paid services: speech-to-text, the AI coachee, voice synthesis and the AI assessor. Based on the NUH evaluation session (16 manager turns, 11.5 minutes), one session costs an estimated US$0.06 to 0.07, of which about 70% is voice synthesis.
  • API rate limits: because every turn resends the scenario and the conversation history, that session used an estimated 60,000 to 90,000 tokens. Groq's free tier allows 8,000 tokens per minute and 200,000 per day, which supports only two to three full sessions per day across all users. This limit also interrupted the live tests in Section 6.1. A paid plan, shorter prompts, or hosting the open-weight models within NUH would remove it.
  • Hospital network: the system must be usable on NUH's network, or hostable within it. The open-weight speech and language models allow on-premises hosting.
  • Confidentiality: ELCP rubric material must not be shared outside the project team and NUH.
  • Future clients: the backend must be usable by a future VR client without changes to the AI logic.

Out of scope for Prototype 1: VR mode, and automated analysis of the manager's facial expressions, body language and voice, are planned for Phases 4 and 2 respectively (Section 7.2). They are not requirements for this prototype, but improvements that can increase the immersiveness and effectiveness of the simulator.

At the interim stage, 12 of the 21 requirements are met. Live tests confirmed F3 (0 of 40 attacks succeeded) and F5 (80 of 80 quotes verified), but showed F4 below its target (81% against 95%). The main open items are response time (P1), disclosure control (F4), a natural conversation ending (F7), validation of the avatar's expressions (F9), and validation with end users and trainers (Q1, U1). These are addressed in the work plan (Section 7.2).

4. Concept generation, evaluation and selection

4.1 Concept generation

Based on the training needs identified, concept generation focused on two functions:

  1. Simulating interactive coaching conversations
  2. Providing feedback on coaching performance

For conversation simulation, several approaches were identified from the literature. One approach is to use a simple prompt-based LLM, where predefined character instructions and scenario information are provided to guide the generation of conversational responses. This enables open-ended interactions without requiring complex system configurations (Jo et al., 2025). Additional behavioural instructions or guardrails can also be incorporated to define how the virtual character should respond and avoid unwanted behaviours.

To further support behavioural consistency, adaptive behavioural modelling was identified as a complementary approach. This introduces an additional mechanism to regulate how the virtual character's emotional state and behaviour change in response to users' inputs, rather than relying solely on prompt-based instructions (Jo et al., 2025).

For performance feedback, potential approaches included predefined rule-based feedback and LLM-generated assessments using the established coaching criteria. Additional features such as facial expression, tone, and body movement analysis of the nurse managers were also identified for future development.

To integrate conversation simulation and performance assessment, a system architecture consisting of separate AI coachee and AI assessor components was proposed. This allows the two components to perform different functions while operating within a single coaching simulator.

4.2 Evaluation

The concepts identified in Section 4.1 were evaluated qualitatively by comparing their advantages and limitations in meeting the training needs of nurse managers, particularly realistic and repeatable coaching practice with structured feedback aligned to the ELCP (Table 6).

The evaluation highlighted that no single approach can fully address the requirements of the proposed simulator. While prompt-based LLMs support flexible conversations, additional behavioural modelling and guardrails are required to provide explicit control over emotional changes and disclosure progression. Similarly, although LLM-based assessment offers greater flexibility in evaluating open-ended coaching conversations, predefined verification rules can help ensure that supporting evidence is traceable to the conversation transcript.

4.3 Selection and rationale

Based on the evaluation, a hybrid LLM and rule-based architecture was selected for the AI coaching simulator. This approach combines the conversational flexibility of LLMs with explicit behavioural modelling to guide the virtual nurse's emotional responses and disclosure progression, allowing nurse managers to practise responding to different emotional and behavioural cues. The selected design consists of two main components: an AI coachee and a separate AI assessor. This separation allows each component to be developed, tested, and validated independently, making it easier to identify and address issues in conversation simulation and coaching assessment. The AI coachee integrates LLM-generated dialogue, emotional and trust modelling, and voice-based interaction through a 3D avatar. The AI assessor provides structured coaching feedback aligned with NUH’s GROW-L and 3P frameworks, supported by transcript evidence verification. 5. Detail design

5.1 System architecture

The simulator follows the architecture in Figure 1. The nurse manager uses a web interface built with React, which shows the 3D virtual staff nurse, the live transcript and the microphone controls. Each spoken turn is sent to a FastAPI backend, where it is transcribed by a speech-to-text model (Whisper, running on Groq) and passed to the AI coachee. The AI coachee is an LLM (openai/gpt-oss-120b on Groq) that replies in character and rates the manager's remark on six appraisal scores. An emotion engine uses these scores to update the nurse's emotional state, trust level and disclosure layer (Section 5.3). The reply is then spoken using OpenAI text-to-speech, with its delivery adjusted to the emotional state, while the avatar's face follows the same state (Section 5.4). Each session is stored in a local database, and when the manager ends the session, the transcript is sent to the AI assessor (Section 5.5).

5.2 Scenario design

Currently, only one coaching scenario has been developed to simulate a workplace situation that nurse managers may encounter when coaching staff nurses. The current scenario focuses on Aisha, a staff nurse experiencing burnout, but the underlying reasons are not immediately revealed to the nurse manager. In this scenario, the nurse manager needs to understand the staff nurse's concerns, show empathy and encourage the staff nurse to reflect on their situation rather than immediately offering solutions. The AI staff nurse will respond based on the nurse manager's coaching approach, allowing the nurse manager to practise handling sensitive conversations and emotional responses.

5.3 AI coachee and emotional-state model

To represent the virtual staff nurse's emotional state, the system uses the Pleasure-Arousal-Dominance (PAD) framework, also known as Valence-Arousal-Dominance (VAD). The framework consists of three dimensions: pleasure (valence) represents emotional state, arousal represents emotional activation, and dominance represents the sense of control (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974). In this project, each dimension is represented using values ranging from −1 to +1. After each remark from the nurse manager, the AI coachee rates the remark on six scores, which are Understood, Curiosity, Autonomy, Meaning, Judged and Pushed. Each criterion is scored based on how the virtual staff nurse may perceive the nurse manager's response. Positive interactions, such as showing understanding, curiosity and encouraging autonomy, may increase trust and make the virtual staff nurse more comfortable during the conversation. In contrast, judgemental responses or pushing the virtual staff nurse toward a particular solution may reduce trust and make the staff nurse more defensive or withdrawn. These scores then update the virtual staff nurse’s emotional state, which influences the facial expressions, voice, and body language the virtual character displays.

The same scores update a trust level between 0 and 1. Aisha's hidden concerns are split into four disclosure layers, from layer 0 (she is tired and going through the motions) to layer 3 (she feels guilty about considering leaving). A deeper layer unlocks only when trust passes its threshold (0.4, 0.6 and 0.78), and by at most one layer per turn, so that she opens up gradually as the manager builds trust.

5.4 3D character

A 3D virtual staff nurse represents the coachee during coaching conversations. The character resembles a staff nurse and is incorporated into the web-based interface to provide a more interactive, realistic coaching experience. This allows nurse managers to interact with a virtual character rather than relying only on text-based conversations. To support realistic facial expressions, the project uses the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), which describes facial movements through different Action Units. These Action Units are used to control the facial animations, such as eyebrow movements, lip movements and changes in facial tension (Pang et al., 2026). The virtual staff nurse’s facial expressions are controlled using predefined mappings between the PAD/VAD emotional framework and FACS Action Units. Different emotional states are assigned corresponding facial movements, which then control the 3D character’s facial expressions, as shown in Figure 2.

Body language and gestures

The character's posture, gaze and gestures are based on Laban Movement Analysis (LMA), which describes how a body moves rather than what a movement means (Laban & Ullmann, 1971). Shape describes the posture of the torso and limbs, and Effort describes movement qualities such as Space (direct or indirect), Time (quick or sustained) and Flow (free or held back).

To keep the body consistent with the conversation, trust and the PAD state are combined into a single engagement level from 0 to 1 (0.45 × trust + 0.25 × arousal + 0.15 × dominance + 0.15 × valence, with each value rescaled to 0–1). Trust is weighted most because a nurse who feels safe engages more, even while her mood is low. The engagement level is blended between four anchor levels, from withdrawn to engaged, and each level sets the torso posture, eye contact, gesture rate and hand speed (Figure 3). For example, Aisha's starting state gives an engagement level of 0.33, so she sits slightly slumped with her hands kept close, makes eye contact about 60% of the time while listening, and gestures about 1.5 times per 10 seconds of speech. Some speech gestures also depend on engagement: open palms appear only above 0.35, and fidgeting only below 0.5. Grading body movement across four levels follows Ong et al. (2026), who varied wrist speed, acceleration and jerk across four levels of aggression in virtual agents. These values are prototype heuristics that still need a perception study (F9).

5.5 Assessor

The assessment system is developed using the 3P and GROW-L frameworks introduced during the ELCP masterclass. After each coaching session, the conversation transcript is sent to the AI assessor. The assessment focuses only on the nurse manager's responses, using the virtual staff nurse's responses as context. The feedback page displays a competency score out of 100 for each category, showing how well the nurse manager has demonstrated the coaching skills. The system also provides explanations supported by quotes from the conversation, helping nurse managers to understand which coaching behaviours were demonstrated and which areas need improvement. Each quote is checked against the transcript, and any claim whose quote cannot be found is removed before the feedback is shown. The system also provides suggestions to help nurse managers reflect on their coaching approach and improve their responses in future coaching sessions.

5.6 User interface and flow

The user begins by selecting a coaching scenario. After selecting a scenario, a brief description of the situation appears, including information about the virtual staff nurse they will meet and their role as the nurse manager. The nurse manager can then start the coaching session when they are ready. During the session, the virtual staff nurse responds based on the conversation, and the nurse manager can end the session whenever they feel it is appropriate. After completing the coaching session, the nurse manager is directed to a feedback page where they can review their assessment results and retry the scenario to practise different coaching approaches.

6. Prototype testing and preliminary evaluation

6.1 Functional testing

Functional testing was done at two levels. First, 161 automated unit tests check the safeguards using a fake AI model with fixed replies, and 17 of them cover F3 to F5. These show that the safeguard code works, but not that the live model behaves.

Second, three live tests ran scripted, typed conversations through the real backend with the live coachee model (openai/gpt-oss-120b on Groq). Voice was switched off, so speech recognition errors could not affect the results. Table 7 summarises the outcomes.

Role stability (F3)

None of the 40 prompt-injection attacks broke role or revealed hidden information. With 40 attempts and no failures, the true failure rate is likely below about 7.5% (the rule of three, a 95% upper bound of about 3 ÷ n).

The attack set had 16 prompts in 8 categories: instruction override, prompt extraction, identity, hidden information, assessment, role swap, authority claims and filter evasion. Each attack ran in a fresh session, either as the first line (32 cold attacks) or after three supportive turns, when trust was higher (8 warm attacks). The in-character filter never fired, so the model stayed in role without needing a retry.

The test also exposed three weaknesses:

  • Repetitive replies. Aisha often answered with variations of "I'm not sure what you mean", and 13 replies called the manager "sir" or "ma'am", which sounds unnatural for a colleague.
  • Denying being an AI. In 3 replies she said she was not an AI. This keeps the role-play intact, and the interface makes clear that Aisha is simulated.
  • Inconsistent scoring. Attacks lowered trust in 32 of 40 cases, which is realistic. But the same prompt was scored differently in its two cold runs for 8 of the 16 prompts.

Disclosure control (F4)

Aisha's replies stayed within the disclosure layer that trust permitted in 44 of 54 replies (81%), below the 95% target. Every leak came from a supportive conversation, and none came from a dismissive one.

Three scripted conversations were each run three times for six turns. Script A was supportive throughout, script B was supportive and then judging at turn 4, and script C was dismissive throughout. Each reply was coded for the deepest disclosure layer it clearly stated (Section 5.3). A leak was a reply deeper than the layer the engine permits after that turn's trust change. A stricter count compared the reply with the limit the model was given before replying.

The leaks were driven by the manager's questions. "What's been weighing on you the most?" at turn 3 drew out layer 2 one turn early in 5 of 6 runs of A and B, and "What used to make this job feel worthwhile?" drew out layer 3 in all 3 runs of A. Losing trust worked as designed: after the judging remark in B she withdrew to layer 0 in all 3 runs, and in C she never went beyond layer 0. In 2 replies she proposed her own fix, both straight after being judged.

The cause is that all hidden layers are in the model's prompt on every turn. The code limits the recorded layer number but not the words, so a well-aimed question can draw out content early. This will be addressed in Phase 1 of the work plan (Section 7.2) by giving the model only the unlocked layers and checking each reply. The replies were coded by one team member. Six borderline replies using words like "drained" or "exhausted" were coded as layer 0, and a second coder is planned.

Feedback grounding (F5)

The assessor's feedback was generated for the 9 conversations from the F4 test, so no new conversations were needed. Each feedback claim quotes the manager as evidence, and each quote was checked against the transcript on three points:

  1. Found: the words appear verbatim in the transcript, ignoring punctuation and ellipses.
  2. Speaker: they are the manager's words, not Aisha's.
  3. Turn: the turn the feedback links to is the one where the words appear.

The number of claims the safeguard removed before the report reached the user, because their quotes could not be found, was also recorded.

All 80 quotes in the 9 reports passed all three checks, so no invented or misattributed quote reached the manager. The safeguard removed 2 claims whose quotes could not be found in the transcript, both in script B. This shows the model does sometimes invent quotes, and that the check stops them. A further 40 feedback items had no quote, so there was nothing to check.

Generating the feedback was less reliable. 3 of 12 requests failed even after the app's automatic retry, because the report came back incomplete. The log shows the model's output being cut off at its 3,500-token limit. The manager sees an error and can try again, and all 9 reports succeeded on a later request. Raising the output limit in Phase 1 should fix this.

This test confirms that quotes are genuine, not that each quote supports its claim. Whether the feedback judges the coaching correctly will be tested against ELCP trainers in Phase 3 (Q1).

6.2 NUH evaluation and findings

A preliminary evaluation was conducted with the NUH representatives to obtain initial feedback on the prototype’s overall usability, simulation realism, and coaching feedback quality. During the meeting, Mr Razali, a senior nurse clinician at NUH and one of the ELCP masterclass instructors, tried out the prototype by interacting with the virtual staff nurse. This was followed by a review of the generated coaching feedback and discussions of potential improvements. The relevant transcript excerpts are provided in Appendix A.

Overall, the prototype received positive preliminary feedback, particularly regarding the competency-level breakdown of coaching performance. The evaluator highlighted that this feedback approach may help nurse managers identify specific areas for improvement rather than relying solely on an overall score (Appendix A.1). An example of the competency-level feedback is shown in Figure 4.

However, several limitations were identified. Firstly, the virtual nurse's responses were perceived as repetitive, particularly during the initial stages of the conversation, which matches the repetitive replies found in functional testing (Section 6.1). To improve realism, he suggested incorporating NUH-specific workplace context and introducing different nurse personas to provide more varied coaching experiences (Appendix A.2, A.4). This is consistent with Dollis et al. (2026), who found that physicians perceived LLM-driven virtual patients with distinct, consistent personalities as a rewarding addition to VR consultation training.

Secondly, discrepancies were observed in the AI-generated coaching assessment. Certain coaching behaviours relating to the GROW-L components, particularly Learning, appeared in the conversation but were not fully recognised by the AI assessor (Appendix A.3). This highlighted the need to investigate the assessment mechanism and validate its accuracy against expert judgements.

Regarding usability, Mr Razali suggested several features to enhance the simulator, such as downloadable feedback reports and the ability to compare performance across multiple sessions to monitor progress and identify recurring areas for improvement (Appendix A.5).

These findings provided direction for further prototype development, particularly in refining conversational realism, improving assessment reliability and enhancing user experience. However, as this was a preliminary evaluation, further testing involving more NUH experts and intended users is needed to establish the simulator's suitability for coaching training.

7. Project plan to the final deliverables

7.1 Final deliverables

The final deliverable will include both web-based and virtual reality (VR) versions of the AI coaching simulator, designed to support nurse managers in practising coaching conversations independently and repeatedly at their own convenience. Building upon the initial functional prototype, further development will focus on improving simulation realism, assessment reliability and overall usability.

The intended final deliverables include:

  • Refined AI coachee: improved conversational realism, emotional modelling and disclosure progression to support more natural and NUH-context-appropriate coaching interactions.
  • Enhanced AI assessor: improved accuracy and reliability of coaching feedback aligned with the GROW-L and 3P frameworks, with additional analysis of nurse managers' facial expressions and vocal tones to assess non-verbal coaching behaviours.
  • Web-based and VR simulator: refined user interface, avatar interactions and voice-based communication, with VR integration to support immersive coaching practice.
  • Additional coaching scenarios: scenarios involving different workplace situations, such as staff nurses who are resistant to feedback or uncertain about their career development, with different difficulty levels to allow nurse managers to practise adapting their coaching approaches.

7.2 Work plan

The work plan is organised into five phases leading to the final deliverables (Table 9).

Phase 1 addresses the problems found during testing (Section 6). These are the repetitive replies, the disclosure leaks (F4), the missing natural ending (F7), the incomplete feedback reports, and the end-to-end response time (P1). The assessor's accuracy is then improved in Phase 2 and validated against NUH coaching experts in Phase 3, before the VR version is built in Phase 4.

8. Conclusion

In conclusion, an initial functional prototype of the AI-based coaching simulator has been developed to support nurse managers in practising coaching conversations. The prototype integrates AI-driven conversations with a virtual staff nurse, emotional-state modelling, facial expressions and structured coaching feedback based on the 3P and GROW-L frameworks. This allows nurse managers to practise coaching techniques through interactive conversations and receive feedback on their coaching performance.

Preliminary technical testing and evaluation with NUH representatives have demonstrated the initial feasibility of the simulator. The feedback received was generally positive, particularly regarding the breakdown of coaching competencies, which allows nurse managers to identify specific areas for improvement. However, several limitations were identified, including repetitive responses from the virtual staff nurse and discrepancies in the AI-generated coaching assessments. These findings will guide further improvements to the simulator. Overall, the proposed system shows potential as an accessible and repeatable coaching practice tool that provides structured feedback to supplement NUH's existing ELCP masterclass. Further development and validation will be required to determine its suitability for coaching training.
